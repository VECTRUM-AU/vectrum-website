01 — Dashboard

The whole radio, at a glance.

One live view per SIM, refreshed on an adaptive poll that backs off when nothing is changing.

The operator line resolves the MVNO against its host network, so an amaysim SIM reads as amaysim on Optus 50502 rather than just "Optus" — and home, roaming and no-service are distinguished rather than merged.

  • Colour-graded metrics — RSRP, RSRQ, SINR and RSSI, each scaled against its own standard range, not a shared five-bar abstraction
  • Timing advance and bandwidth read from the serving cell where the modem reports them
  • EN-DC secondary layer shown alongside the anchor with SS-RSRP, SS-RSRQ and SS-SINR
  • MOS quality score for voice, data and text, derived from the live radio conditions
02 — Cells · Serving

Decoded, not displayed.

Android hands an app a raw cell identity. RAZUM takes it apart.

The LTE cell ID splits into eNB and sector, with the raw CID kept visible beside it. EARFCN resolves to a band and a real downlink frequency in MHz. On NR, the NCI splits into gNB ID and cell index, and the NR-ARFCN resolves to band and frequency the same way.

Where Android or the modem refuses a value, RAZUM says which — Modem Restriction, or not exposed by Android for NR cells — instead of printing a zero and letting you believe it.

  • LTE — eNB ID, sector, raw CID, PCI, TAC, MCC-MNC, PLMN, EARFCN, band, DL frequency, bandwidth, timing advance
  • 5G NR — gNB ID, cell index, raw NCI, PCI, NR-ARFCN, band, DL frequency, SS-RSRP, SS-RSRQ, SS-SINR, CSI-RSRP
  • UMTS and GSM — RNC/CID, PSC, UARFCN, RSCP, Ec/No · LAC/CID, BSIC, ARFCN
  • Honest gaps — every unavailable field carries the reason it is unavailable
03 — Cells · Neighbours

What else the radio can see.

Every neighbour the modem reports, with the identity decoded the same way as the serving cell.

Code, band, channel, radio node and signal strength in one table, with a live timeline underneath showing how the set has changed over a rolling five-minute window. PCI conflicts are called out where two cells on the same channel share a physical cell ID.

  • Per-RAT neighbour decode — PCI/PSC/BSIC, band, channel, radio node
  • Live timeline over the last rolling window, not a single snapshot
  • PCI conflict detection, separating same-channel from different-channel cases
04 — Cells · Band Plots

Every carrier, plotted live.

Carrier aggregation and EN-DC make "the signal" a set of carriers, not a number.

Band Plots lists each active carrier with its ARFCN, centre frequency and duplex mode, then plots RSRP, SINR, RSRQ and NR SS-RSRP as live traces with minimum, maximum and current value over a rolling window — so a carrier that drops in and out is visible as a shape, not as a flicker you had to be watching for.

  • Per-carrier listing — band, ARFCN, centre frequency, FR1/FR2, active state
  • Live traces for RSRP, SINR, RSRQ and NR SS-RSRP with min / max / now
  • Rolling window so short-lived behaviour is still on screen when you look
05 — Quadar

Interference, by derivation.

A weak signal and a jammed signal look identical on a signal bar. They are not the same problem, and they do not have the same fix.

3GPP defines SINR over the same resource elements as RSRP. That makes interference-plus-noise equal to RSRP minus SINR — exactly, as an identity, not as a model with a fudge factor. Quadar computes that figure and plots it against the receiver's own thermal noise floor, so the centre of each face is the floor and every ring out from it is a decibel the receiver is losing to something other than distance.

Co-channel neighbours are placed on the same radius axis by the contribution each one individually makes to that shortfall, which turns "there is interference" into "this PCI is responsible for this many decibels of it".

  • Two faces — the LTE anchor and the NR layer, each judged on its own floor and subcarrier spacing
  • Two modes — shortfall above the thermal floor, or signed SINR on a scale that goes negative where the radio does
  • Four verdicts — clean, coverage-limited, loaded, interference-limited
  • Co-channel attribution — each visible neighbour's own contribution in dB, by PCI
  • Self-checking — a shortfall below the thermal floor is reported as an implied ceiling on the assumed receiver noise figure, because the assumption is the weaker claim, not the radio
06 — Events

What happened, and when.

A timeline of real radio events, determined from state changes rather than inferred from a dropped bar.

Calls are classified by the technology that actually carried them — VoLTE, VoNR, VoWiFi, or CS on GSM or UMTS — with CS fallback and SRVCC distinguished from one another instead of lumped together. When a call ends abnormally, RAZUM correlates against the PDN bearer state to separate my end from other reasons, which is the difference between a complaint and a finding.

  • Mobility — handovers, radio link failure, reverse fallback, roaming start and end
  • Carriers — CA component added and dropped, NR layer added and dropped, EN-DC fallback to LTE-only
  • Voice — call start, end, drop and handover with the bearer technology named
  • RF findings — PCI conflict, co-channel interference, signal tier transitions
  • Manual incidents you mark yourself, timestamped into the same stream
07 — Map

Your coverage and your plan, on the same map.

Every recorded measurement is geo-tagged, so your sessions become a map of the coverage you have actually had.

No crowdsourcing and no backend: the heatmap is built on this device from this device's own recordings. Choose the scope — all time, the last 7 or 30 days, or a specific set of sessions — and the bins recompute against it.

Two metrics are available. Signal normalises every row against its own RAT's range before averaging, so a bin that mixes an LTE pass with a 5G pass still means something. Interference runs the same rows through the Quadar derivation and maps the shortfall instead, which puts the two tools on the same geography.

And it is not only your measurements. Import your own site plan as a CSV — export a template from the app, fill it in, import it back — and the map draws one pin per site and a wedge per sector. The sector actually carrying you is matched by cell ID, or by channel and code, and lit in brand teal against the rest, with a line drawn from you to it.

That is the question a plan exists to answer: not where coverage is supposed to be, but which planned sector you are standing in right now.

  • On-device only — your recordings, never uploaded
  • Scope — all time, last 7 days, last 30 days, or chosen sessions
  • Two metrics — signal quality or derived interference shortfall
  • Site plan overlay — site pins and azimuth sector wedges from your own CSV
  • Live sector match — the planned sector you are on, highlighted
  • Live cell layers — serving cell pin, neighbour pins, and a timing-advance distance ring
  • Serving cell card with operator, eNB/gNB, sector, PLMN, TAC, PCI and live RSRP/SINR
08 — Speed test

A number is not a result.

A throughput figure with no RF context is unfalsifiable. RAZUM captures the conditions that produced it, and then says whether the figure is reasonable.

The test binds to the cellular radio even when Wi-Fi is connected, so the result reflects the layer you meant to test. Alongside download, upload and latency, RAZUM records the serving configuration at test time — the layer carrying the data, its bandwidth, the SINR, the connected cells — and compares the measured rate against what that configuration could carry.

When the measured figure sits below the estimate, that is stated as a finding about the network or the server, not quietly hidden.

  • Transport binding — force the test onto cellular, or test Wi-Fi deliberately
  • Throughput assessment — measured against a 3GPP peak for the carrier and against what the measured SINR supports
  • Latency under load — round trip, jitter, min/max and connection setup, idle and during transfer
  • Network path — DNS resolver, Private DNS state, resolved address, MTU, local address and interface
  • RF context captured with the result, including the NR secondary layer and the connected cell identities
  • Recent tests kept with their transport and conditions, so results stay comparable
09 — Network diagnostics

Ping, traceroute, and where you actually are.

The transport-layer tools, on the same radio you have just been measuring.

Ping reports loss and round-trip time per echo rather than an average with no spread. Traceroute shows each hop with its address and per-hop timing, so a problem can be placed somewhere rather than just observed. Every result can be shared as plain text, with the host and the time it ran already in the header.

  • Ping — per-echo sequence, loss percentage, average and min/max round trip
  • Traceroute — per-hop address and timing, one probe per hop
  • My IP — the address the network actually presents for you
  • Shareable output — every tab exports as text with host and timestamp
10 — Sessions & export

Record it, then take it with you.

A session records every measurement with its position, its cell identity and the events that occurred, and keeps running in the background with a live notification while it does.

Sessions are named, searchable and summarised — measurement count, events, incidents, distance covered, and average and minimum MOS. Recorded routes can be replayed with a metric scrubber, and the whole session exports in the format your workflow already uses.

  • Background recording with a live notification showing the current serving cell and signal
  • Per-session summary — measurements, events, incidents, distance, average and minimum MOS
  • Session playback — replay the route with a metric scrubber
  • Export — CSV, JSON, KML for Google Earth, or a full HTML report
  • Scheduled reports — daily, weekly or monthly PDF digests, generated on the device
